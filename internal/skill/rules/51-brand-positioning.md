# Rule: Brand Positioning

**Domain:** branding · **ID:** `51-brand-positioning`

## Purpose
Converts strategy into one written sentence that can accept or reject a design option. If a positioning statement cannot kill a layout, it is a paragraph of intent, not a constraint.

## Definition
A positioning statement names the audience, the need, the category the brand competes in, the single benefit it delivers, and the proof that makes the benefit believable: "For [audience] who [need], [brand] is the [category] that [benefit], because [proof]." Each clause maps to a design decision — category sets the visual reference set, benefit sets emphasis, proof sets what must be shown rather than claimed.

## Core Principles
- The category chooses the comparison set. Use the category the audience already uses; if you invent one, you owe them an explanation in every layout.
- One primary benefit, two supports at most. Three equal benefits position nothing, because the viewer cannot rank what the designer did not.
- Proof must be checkable: a number, a process, a material, a person, a date. "Quality" and "passion" are not proof.
- The swap test: replace your brand name with a competitor's. If the sentence still reads true, it is not positioning.
- Positioning constrains form. "Fastest" produces dense, high-contrast, motion-led work; "most careful" produces wide margins, two colors and long-form evidence.
- Write it before the moodboard. A statement written after the visual direction is a caption for a decision already made.

## Techniques
1. **Fill the template in 35 words or fewer** — longer statements hide the disagreement rather than resolving it.
2. **Write the anti-statement** — "we are not ___" for three named competitors, plus three things you refuse to be. Refusals are more actionable than ambitions.
3. **Benefit ladder** — feature, then functional benefit, then emotional benefit. Design usually carries the emotional level with type and color, and the functional level with diagrams and numbers.
4. **Proof inventory** — list every asset that proves the claim: photographs, data, certifications, named clients. Fewer than two assets means the claim is a wish.
5. **Constraint table** — for each clause write one "must" and one "must not" for the design. That table is the handoff artifact.

## When to Use
- Before a new identity, a campaign, a pitch deck, or a website rewrite.
- When a design review stalls because two options are both "nice" — the statement breaks the tie.
- When sales and design describe the product differently.

## When to Avoid
- Single-asset tactical work where the positioning already exists and is not in question.
- Early exploration, where fixing the sentence too soon narrows the search before the material is understood.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Benefit stated as "quality" | Unfalsifiable and undrawable | Name a measurable outcome ("4-day proofs") |
| Two audiences in one sentence | Cannot be designed for both | Split into a primary statement and a secondary note |
| Inventing a category | Requires teaching before selling | Use the audience's category, differentiate inside it |
| Proof written as an adjective | Nothing to show on the page | Replace with a number, name, or artifact |

## Practical Examples
Thirty-four words: "For studio owners who lose weekends to admin, Ledgerly is the bookkeeping service that closes the month in four days, because every account is handled by one named bookkeeper." Design consequences: the four-day claim becomes a repeated three-step timeline band with numerals at 28 px; "one named bookkeeper" requires a real portrait and first names on the page; "studios" sets the photographic reference to workspaces rather than stock handshakes; the anti-statement "not a marketplace" removes star ratings and browse-all grids.
