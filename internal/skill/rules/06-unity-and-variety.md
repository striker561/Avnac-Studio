# Rule: Unity and Variety

**Domain:** foundations · **ID:** `06-unity-and-variety`

## Purpose
Hold a composition together as one object while keeping enough difference that it stays alive. Without unity the parts read as a collection; without variety they read as a template.

## Definition
Unity is the evidence, visible at a glance, that all parts belong to the same thing. Variety is controlled difference that keeps a unified field from going inert. Unity comes from repeating properties; variety from varying exactly one of them on purpose.

## Core Principles
- Unify by property, not by duplication: one family, one palette, one corner radius, one stroke weight, one image treatment, one light direction.
- Aim for roughly 80% shared properties and 20% deviation. Below that reads as chaos; above, as monotone.
- One wild card per composition. Two deviant elements stop being accents and become a second system.
- Photographic treatment is the cheapest unifier: a single duotone or grade makes unrelated images belong together.
- A background plate, tint, or common crop binds illustrations that share no style.
- Variety must not change what the reader understands. A varied property should signal a difference in content, or it is noise.

## Techniques
1. **Fix four tokens first** — family (Inter), palette (`#0E0E0E`, `#F5F1E8`, `#D8FF3E`), radius (24 px), stroke (2 px). Reuse them in every element.
2. **Vary one property** — pick scale, rotation, texture, or color; vary exactly one, in exactly one place.
3. **Grade the images** — apply the same duotone (`#101820` / `#F2AA4C`) and 15% grain to every photo in the set.
4. **Bind with ground** — put disparate cutouts on one tint plate or halftone panel so the group reads as a single illustration.
5. **Drift audit** — count distinct font sizes, hex values, radii, and stroke weights. More than three in any category is drift, not variety.

## When to Use
- Multi-panel sets, carousels, decks, campaign systems, anything seen in series or beside siblings.
- When a set of images comes from different sources and must look commissioned together.
- When a set feels like separate designs that happen to share a folder.

## When to Avoid
- A single small piece, where unity is trivial and variety does the work.
- Deliberately plural aesthetics — postmodern collage, Memphis — where clash is the concept. Then unify with one hard rule, usually a grid or a palette, and break the rest.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Unity by making everything identical | Monotone; nothing indicates where to look | Vary one property at the focal item |
| Variety by adding a new typeface | Reads as inconsistency, not contrast | Same family, different weight |
| Texture added to hide weak structure | Noise on top of a weak skeleton | Fix alignment and scale first |
| Two accents competing | Neither registers | One wild card, chosen by message |
| One photo left untreated | That image reads as an error | Treat all or none |

## Practical Examples
A six-card carousel at 1080×1080. Shared: 64 px margins, 24 px radius, Inter, palette `#0E0E0E`, `#F5F1E8`, `#D8FF3E`, and every photo duotoned.

Variety: card 4 turns its label 90° and sets it at 96 px against 24 px everywhere else. One wild card, on the card whose content is the turn. At 270 px the set reads as one system with one interruption, which is exactly the intent.
