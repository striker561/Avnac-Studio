# Rule: Type Classification

**Domain:** typography · **ID:** `21-type-classification`

## Purpose
Narrow a font choice from hundreds to a handful using construction and history rather than mood, and know what each category promises before you test it on the canvas.

## Definition
Classification groups typefaces by how they were built: serif construction, stress axis, stroke contrast, and the period that produced them. The working set is serif (Venetian, Garalde, Transitional, Didone), slab, sans (grotesque, neo-grotesque, humanist, geometric), script, display, and monospace.

## Core Principles
- Classify by three features, not by vibe: serif construction (bracketed, unbracketed, hairline), stress axis (oblique or vertical), and stroke contrast (monoline or high).
- Dates carry associations. Garalde 1530s, Transitional 1750s, Didone 1790s, slab 1810s, grotesque 1890s, geometric 1927, humanist sans 1928–1976, neo-grotesque 1957.
- Categories are not personalities. A Didone reads as fashion and luxury because of 150 years of use, not because hairlines mean elegance.
- Text and display are size regimes, not categories: a display cut has tighter spacing, finer hairlines, and smaller counters, and it fails below about 24 px.
- One or two categories per design. A third is usually a role a weight would have covered.

## Techniques
1. **Read the serif.** Bracketed and oblique with moderate contrast reads humanist or Garalde; thin, unbracketed, and vertical reads Didone; thick and blocky reads slab.
2. **Match category to distance.** Poster and headline: display, slab, or grotesque. Long text: Garalde or Transitional for print, humanist or neo-grotesque sans for screen.
3. **Choose the era deliberately.** A 1962 Swiss reference wants a neo-grotesque (Helvetica, Univers, or a modern stand-in such as Inter); a Didone puts the sheet in 1798 or in a fashion magazine.
4. **Check the cut, not just the family.** Text cut for 9–12 pt, display cut for 36 px and up, optical-size axis where the family has one (see 29).

## When to Use
- Choosing a family for an unfamiliar brief.
- Building a period reference that has to survive a knowing client.
- Explaining why a fashionable serif is wrong for a 10 pt caption block.

## When to Avoid
- Don't use classification to predict quality; a grotesque can be badly drawn. Judge the drawing (see 22).
- Don't mix three categories to look eclectic; eclecticism needs a system behind it.
- Don't reach for a script or a display face for body copy, however good it looks at 72 px.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Serif = traditional, sans = modern" | Helvetica is 1957; a Didone is 1798 | Read the construction, not the label |
| Display cut at 14 px | Hairlines break up and counters fill in | Use the text cut |
| Slab at 9 pt body | The slabs thicken the line and darken the page | Slab at 12 pt+ or headline only |
| Script in all caps | Connections break; the word stops reading as one shape | Lowercase only, 24 px and up |
| Monospace for paragraphs | 10–15% wider, with an uneven gray | Mono for code, tables, and labels |

## Practical Examples
An A2 poster for a 1962 Swiss exhibition: neo-grotesque at three sizes — 112 px headline, 28 px subhead, 18 px details — with −2% tracking on the headline and ragged right throughout. A Didone headline was tried first and rejected: it read as a perfume advertisement, and its hairline serifs disappeared at the 3 m distance the sheet is designed for.
