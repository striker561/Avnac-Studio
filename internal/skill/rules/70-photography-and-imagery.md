# Rule: Photography and Imagery

**Domain:** imagery · **ID:** `70-photography-and-imagery`

## Purpose
Pick or brief the picture that carries the claim, and know what you may legally do with it. Imagery is the
most literal element in most layouts: it states the subject, so a generic photograph undoes a correct design
faster than any typographic error.

## Definition
Imagery selection matches subject, light, authenticity, and licence to the brief. Commissioning is the same
decision made in advance: you specify the photograph before it exists and buy rights sized to where it runs.

## Core Principles
- Test at 120 px first. An image that only works at full size is a texture, not a subject; if the meaning
  disappears in a thumbnail, change the crop or the choice.
- Choose light that serves the layout: know which side the light falls on so type lands in a quiet zone
  instead of fighting a highlight.
- Prefer authenticity over polish — people doing the thing, in the place, with the tools. Posed-at-camera
  stock reads as stock within one second.
- Keep one register per layout: do not mix reportage, studio white cyc, and 3D render unless the mismatch is
  the point.
- Treat rights as a design constraint: media, term, territory, print run. A licence expiring in 12 months
  rules out the poster you are designing.

## Techniques
1. **Write the shot brief** — three reference frames, lens (35 mm environmental, 85 mm portrait), one light
   at 45°, wardrobe, which side stays empty for type, and the final aspect ratio.
2. **Search by the crop you need** — search scene plus action plus "copy space left", collect six
   candidates, place all six at final crop and size, then choose.
3. **Licence check** — confirm media, term, territory, print run, allowed modifications, and model and
   property releases before the layout is approved; [`69-image-manipulation`](69-image-manipulation.md)
   covers which alterations the licence must permit.
4. **Set audit** — lay 6–12 images side by side: temperatures within ±300 K, one light direction, consistent
   casting, similar grain. Differences read as a mistake, not as variety.

## When to Use
- Campaigns, editorial, packaging, and any layout where a real person, product, or place is the claim.
- When a client supplies images from several sources and they must look like one shoot.
- When a shoot is cheaper than licensing one specific frame — commissioning wins whenever the claim is specific.

## When to Avoid
- Abstract concepts (growth, trust, integration) where stock defaults to cliché; type or illustration argues
  better — see [`76-visual-metaphors-and-symbolism`](76-visual-metaphors-and-symbolism.md).
- Editorial-only stock for advertising, and any image whose release you cannot produce.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing on the contact sheet | Composition breaks at the real crop | Place candidates at final size and crop |
| Mixing light directions | The layout reads as two scenes | Match or grade to one key direction |
| Editorial licence, commercial use | Legal exposure, takedowns after print | Check the licence for advertising use |
| Stock metaphor as the whole idea | Handshake, lightbulb, puzzle read as filler | Use a specific scene from the client's world |

## Practical Examples
A 148×210 mm flyer puts a portrait in the right column, 68 mm wide, so the subject must sit left of center
with 40% empty space camera-right. The brief asks for 35 mm framing at f/2.8, one 45° softbox, and a
`#8A8A86` wall so the `#111111` headline holds 12:1 contrast in the left column. Three candidates are tested
at 68×92 mm; only one keeps the face readable and the wall empty. Licensed for print and digital, 24 months,
North America, 10,000 units.
