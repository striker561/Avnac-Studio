# Rule: Visual Metaphors and Symbolism

**Domain:** psychology · **ID:** `76-visual-metaphors-and-symbolism`

## Purpose
Choose a metaphor the audience already owns, so an abstract claim becomes a picture that decodes in one step.
Metaphor is how a design argues without a paragraph of copy.

## Definition
A metaphor substitutes a concrete image for an abstract claim: a bridge for connection, a ladder for
advancement. A symbol is a sign read without inference because a culture taught it — a flag, an arrow, a cross.

## Core Principles
- Allow one step of inference and one metaphor per composition. Literal object → metonym (the tool of the trade)
  → metaphor (scale for growth) → symbol (a flag); each extra step halves the audience that arrives.
- Test the mapping: name two or three attributes the claim actually means, then check the image carries at least
  two. One attribute is decoration, not an argument.
- Know your symbols before placing them — flags, hands, eyes, keys, arrows, and crosses carry fixed meaning and
  fixed politics. Check the market, not your own reading.
- Treat the cliché list as a stop sign: lightbulb for idea, handshake for partnership, puzzle for integration,
  gear for engineering, target for goals, rocket for growth, circuit-brain for AI. A cliché announces that no
  thinking was spent.
- Check culture before wit: colors, animals, gestures, and numbers travel badly. Red, white, owls, thumbs-up, the
  number four, and open palms all change meaning between markets.

## Techniques
1. **Metaphor ladder** — write the claim as one verb ("protects", "ships", "remembers"), list that verb's literal
   objects, then its metonyms, then its metaphors. Choose the most concrete option that still carries two
   attributes.
2. **Cliché swap** — replace the stock object with the specific tool of the client's world: a baker's lame for
   craft, a specific valve for engineering, a loading gauge for logistics.
3. **Mapping table** — three claim attributes in one column, the visual evidence for each in the next. A blank
   cell is a gap the viewer will fill with the wrong meaning.
4. **Caption test** — if the image needs a caption to be understood, the metaphor failed; if a caption would
   change its meaning, the metaphor is ambiguous.

## When to Use
- Abstract claims — trust, speed, security, growth, memory — where there is nothing to photograph.
- Covers, campaign key visuals, and posters that must argue in one glance.
- Marks and mascots that need a story beyond the name.

## When to Avoid
- Technical and B2B material where the audience wants the product, the spec, or the diagram.
- Regulated or sacred symbols: flags, medical crosses, religious iconography, currency, official seals.
- Multilingual audiences when the metaphor depends on one language's idiom.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Two metaphors in one image | Each weakens the other, reading stalls | Keep one, cut the second |
| Cliché object as the whole idea | Reads as filler, no claim is made | Swap for a specific tool of the trade |
| Symbol used outside its culture | Insult, ban, or unintended politics | Check markets before artwork |
| Metaphor needing a caption | The image is not doing the work | Rebuild from the claim attributes |

## Practical Examples
A 1080×1350 px poster for a locksmith argues "we know your door". The metaphor is one unbroken `#111111` line
that draws a keyhole and continues into a doorway outline — one object, one step, no lightbulb and no padlock
clip-art. Three attributes map: precision (the line never doubles), continuity (one stroke), fit (the shape closes
only at the keyhole). The 6 px line sits on `#F2EDE4`, with the headline "Every door is different" 120 px below
it, so the image finishes the sentence the type starts.
