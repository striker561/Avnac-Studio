# Rule: Sequence and Pacing

**Domain:** layout · **ID:** `77-sequence-and-pacing`

## Purpose
Compose the order of pages, spreads, or slides as one sequence with a score, so a long document
has rhythm instead of forty independent layouts that each try to be the best page.

## Definition
Sequence is the design of turns: what a viewer sees before and after each view, how dense each
view is, and what each turn reveals. The spread is the static unit; the turn is the unit that is
experienced. A publication is "simultaneously the static experience of a spread and the cinematic
experience of a sequence of pages" (p. 84).

## Core Principles
- The turn is the unit. Compose what the reader sees when the page moves, not only what is on the
  page when it is still.
- Every sequence has beats: establish, develop, rest, reveal, close. Name the beat of a spread
  before designing it, or you are deciding sequence by accident.
- Density alternates. Two or three dense spreads earn one that breathes; a run of identical density
  stops being read as emphasis and starts being read as a wall.
- One reveal per sequence. The largest image, the only full bleed, the single color page — spend
  it at the emotional peak. Repeated reveals cancel each other.
- The layout should disappear. "If you see the layout, it is probably a bad layout" (p. 84): the
  variety you add is in service of the content, not evidence of the designer's range.
- Consistency of furniture makes variation legible. A fixed folio, margin, and baseline system lets
  a bigger image read as an event rather than an accident (see `56-brand-consistency`).
- A cross-spread image is a sequence decision with a binding precondition: it only works where the
  binding opens flat (see `88-binding-and-book-construction`).

## Techniques
1. **Write a beat sheet, one row per spread** — beat, density %, image size, type role, and the
   reward for the turn. Thirty-two rows fit on one page and can be argued about before any layout
   exists.
2. **Review in pairs, not pages** — flip 1-2, 2-3, 3-4 as the reader will. A right-hand page is the
   reward for the turn; if it only continues the left page, the turn has no job.
3. **Set a density rhythm** — three levels: open below 25% active area, medium 35–50%, dense
   60–75%. Never run three dense spreads in a row; never put two reveals adjacent.
4. **Budget the reveal** — one full-bleed spread, one color surprise, and one scale jump per
   16-page signature, placed at the emotional peak rather than evenly spaced.
5. **Bridge the gutter only where the binding allows** — full-spread images on lay-flat or sewn
   binding; on perfect binding design each page to stand and keep faces 10 mm clear of the fold.
6. **Adapt the score to screens** — slides are turns without paper: one idea per slide, a density
   change every three or four slides, and a section or blank slide as a rest beat rather than
   filling every frame.

## When to Use
- Books, brochures, zines, reports, catalogues, lookbooks, programmes, and slide decks — anything
  read as a series of turns.
- When a document reads as tiring even though every spread is well composed: the problem is the
  score, not the pages.
- When adding pages for a client: decide what beat the new page occupies before designing it.

## When to Avoid
- Single-sheet work — a poster is one beat, and pacing it is composition (see `34-composition-techniques`).
- Interface flows, where "sequence" means interaction states and the rules of state design apply
  (see `94-interaction-states`).
- Reference material meant to be jumped around rather than read in order: a price list or a spec
  sheet wants navigation, not a reveal.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Designing every spread as a poster | Each page competes; the whole reads as shouting | Assign beats; let most spreads be medium |
| One density for forty pages | The eye stops registering anything | Alternate open, medium, dense |
| A reveal every third spread | Nothing is a reveal when everything is | One reveal per signature, at the peak |
| Planning the sequence after the layouts | Rework costs more than the beat sheet | Beat sheet before the first spread |
| A cross-gutter hero on perfect binding | The subject disappears into the fold | Single-page image, or resew for flat opening |

## Practical Examples
A 32-page A5 programme, saddle stitched. The beat sheet runs: p1 cover (open), p2–3 greeting
(open), p4–7 schedule (dense), p8–9 portrait (reveal: full-bleed duotone), p10–11 notes (medium),
p12–13 sponsors (open), p14–15 credits (medium), p16–17 centre panorama (reveal two: the only
cross-gutter image, placed where the binding opens flattest), p18–23 talks (dense), p24–25 break
(open), p26–31 listings (dense), p32 colophon (close).

The density pattern is open, open, dense, reveal, medium, open — no three dense spreads in a row,
and the two reveals sit four spreads apart, at the opening quarter and the centre. The panorama is
the only image that crosses the fold; every other image is composed to stand within its page.
