# Rule: Cropping and Framing

**Domain:** imagery · **ID:** `38-cropping-and-framing`

## Purpose
Decide what the frame excludes. A crop is an editorial act: it decides what the reader can infer,
where the subject sits in the composition, and whether an image was chosen or merely placed. Most
weak layouts are weak at the crop, not at the type.

## Definition
A crop is the rectangle through which an image is seen, defined by its aspect ratio, the subject's
position inside it, and where the cut lines fall on the subject's body or the scene's structure.
Framing is the same decision extended to the whole composition: what the canvas includes and what
it pushes past the edge.

## Core Principles
- Aspect ratio comes first. 1:1 for social grids, 4:5 for feed, 16:9 for screens and slides, 3:2 or
  4:3 for most camera sensors. Cropping to a ratio the material does not have creates dead space or
  an amputated subject.
- Never cut through a joint. Crop at mid-thigh, mid-forearm, or mid-torso — a cut at a wrist, knee,
  or neck reads as violence rather than as framing.
- Leave look-space. A face or moving subject needs 1.5–2× more space in front of it than behind;
  the reverse makes the subject look trapped against the edge.
- A crop can be the concept. Removing the face and keeping the hands, or cutting the product at the
  frame edge, is an editorial position — state it and commit rather than cropping timidly.
- Bleed is a separate decision from crop. An image that reaches the trim needs 3 mm of bleed in
  print, and the subject still needs 8–10% clearance inside the trim or a trim shift will clip it.

## Techniques
1. **Shoot the crop ladder** — from one image produce three: 16:9 (wide, establishes place), 4:5
   (mid, subject and context), 1:1 (tight, subject only). One asset then serves three placements
   without re-cropping under deadline.
2. **Place the dominant eye on the upper third** — for a single figure, put the eye at 33% from the
   top and 33% or 66% across. This works because it is where the reader looks first, not because of
   a rule about beauty.
3. **Anchor on a structural line** — find the horizon, wall edge, or table edge and put it at 1/3 or
   2/3 of the frame height. A horizon at exactly 50% halves the image and kills the depth.
4. **Cut again, then again** — after the first crop, remove another 15–25%. The tightest crop that
   still identifies the subject is usually the one that holds a layout; the loose crop is the
   one that leaves the designer arranging furniture.
5. **Bleed with intent** — on a 1080×1350 canvas, an image at x −40 to 1120 and y −40 to 1390
   bleeds 40 px on all four sides. Keep the subject's face 120 px inside the canvas edge and let
   only background reach the trim.

## When to Use
- Every time an image enters a layout, and every time the same image must appear at a second size
  or ratio.
- When a layout feels weak but the type is right — check the crop before changing the type.
- When a subject is small in a wide frame: cropping in is almost always the correction.

## When to Avoid
- Documentary and reportage contexts where the full frame is the evidence; cropping to a nicer
  composition can change what the photograph claims.
- Images whose meaning is in the whole — a wide landscape, an architectural elevation, a group
  portrait — where a tight crop destroys the subject.
- Do not crop and scale in the same move on a low-resolution file: cropping a 1200 px image to 25%
  and then placing it at 800 px wide produces 1.5× upsampling and visible softness.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Horizon through the subject's head | The strongest line in the frame decapitates the subject | Move the horizon to 1/3 or 2/3, or reframe |
| Subject centred with equal space all round | Reads as a passport photo; no direction | Give 1.5–2× look-space in the facing direction |
| Cropping at a wrist or ankle | The cut looks like an injury | Crop mid-limb, 20–30% further along |
| Placing a 600 px image at 900 px wide | Upsampling softens edges and skin | Use the native size, or source a larger file |
| Subject touching the trim edge | Any trim shift clips the subject | Keep 8–10% clearance inside the trim |

## Practical Examples
A 16:9 master photograph of a ceramics studio, 2400×1350 px, placed three ways:

- **16:9 hero** on a 1920×1080 site banner — image at 0,0 scaled to 1920×1080; the potter's hands
  sit at x 780–1100, the wheel's edge at y 900. Horizon (the shelf behind) at y 360, one third.
- **4:5 feed** at 1080×1350 — the same file cropped to x 300–1380 of the master so the hands land
  at 33% across and the shelf edge at 33% down. Cut at mid-forearm, not at the wrist.
- **1:1 avatar** at 400×400 — a tight crop on the hands and the rim of the wheel only, no face, no
  shelf. At this size the hands are the subject; the context would be noise.

The bleed version for print: A5 at 300 ppi is 148×210 mm plus 3 mm bleed on all four sides, and the
potter's hands are held at least 8 mm inside the trim so a 2 mm trim shift does not clip them.
