# Rule: Brand Strategy

**Domain:** branding · **ID:** `50-brand-strategy`

## Purpose
Fixes what a brand must mean to a specific audience before anything is drawn, so the identity has a job to do rather than a look to dress. Use it at the start of identity work, and whenever a client asks for "a fresh look" without saying what should change.

## Definition
Brand strategy is the set of decisions that determine what the brand must mean — and must not mean: audience, category, differentiation, and one organizing idea. The identity is the visible proof of those decisions, not a substitute for them. Strategy constrains design; it never specifies a color.

## Core Principles
- A brand is reputation, not a logo. Shaughnessy's warning is exact: organizations "are not brands; they are TV shows, candy bars, and countries" — branding describes what people already believe, it does not install a belief (p. 93).
- One audience, not an average. Name the primary segment by behavior — "first-time founders hiring their first designer" — not by age band.
- Differentiation must be true and expensive to copy. A claim a competitor could make tomorrow is not a position.
- The brand idea is one sentence of ten words or fewer that a designer can draw. "Precision instruments" draws differently from "friendly helper".
- The identity must express three things: onlyness, personality, proof. Anything it says that strategy does not support is noise the viewer pays for.
- Strategy ends in constraints, not adjectives. If the brief says "make it premium", the strategy work is not finished.

## Techniques
1. **Two-by-two** — plot three to five competitors on the two axes the audience actually cares about (price vs. hand-holding). Find the empty quadrant, then check whether it is empty because it is unavailable or because nobody wants it.
2. **Onlyness statement** — "Our brand is the only ___ that ___." If a competitor fits the blank, revise until none does.
3. **Three attributes, ranked** — pick three, order them, delete any that cannot be shown in a layout. "Confident, warm, exact" beats ten adjectives.
4. **Audience day-in-the-life** — write the three moments the audience meets the design (search result, shelf, invoice) and what must be understood in each.
5. **Strategy-to-design table** — one row per attribute: attribute, visual carrier, proof point, anti-pattern. The anti-pattern column is what stops drift.

## When to Use
- Before any identity work: logo, palette, type, photography, campaign.
- When a rebrand is requested but nobody can name what is wrong with the current one.
- When two stakeholders want opposite things and the argument is really about audience.

## When to Avoid
- Tactical one-offs — a single event flyer — where inherited guidelines already answer every question.
- When there is no product truth to differentiate on. Fix the offer first; an identity built on nothing is decoration with a rationale attached.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Three audiences in one brief | The design pleases all three and signals none | One primary, one secondary, explicitly ranked |
| "Premium but affordable" | Two opposing positions cancel out | Choose the axis and accept the cost |
| Attributes with no visual translation | Cannot be built, cannot be judged | Add carrier and anti-pattern per attribute |
| A moodboard presented as strategy | Moodboards show preferences, not decisions | Write the idea in one sentence first |

## Practical Examples
A two-person letterpress studio. Audience: design-led couples booking 6–12 months ahead. Category: craft stationery. Difference: press work done in-house with 10-day proofs. Idea: "printed, not pictured." Constraints that follow: paper-forward photography at 1:1 and 4:5 crops; one ink plus one paper color, no gradients or gloss; type at 11/16 px minimum in the price list; every layout shows a real press sheet, never a mockup render.
