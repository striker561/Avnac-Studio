# Rule: Rebranding and Evolution

**Domain:** branding · **ID:** `58-rebranding-and-evolution`

## Purpose
Decide whether to redesign at all, then migrate without throwing away the recognition the brand already paid for.

## Definition
A rebrand replaces part of the identity system; a refresh changes its execution. The decision should follow a
diagnosed mismatch between what the brand looks like and what it needs to be believed about.

## Core Principles
- Diagnose in three buckets: recognition (nobody knows you — a visibility problem), relevance (they read you as
  dated or wrong-category), execution (identity fine, applications bad). Only relevance justifies a rebuild.
- Refresh versus rebuild: a refresh keeps logotype geometry and changes type, color, layout, and application; a
  rebuild changes the mark, often the name and architecture. Time and cost differ by roughly 3×–10×.
- Inventory equity before designing. List what carries recognition — silhouette, color, wordmark rhythm, tagline,
  layout habit — and carry at least two of the top three through the change.
- Transition, don't switch. Run a transitional lockup on high-salience surfaces for 6–24 months and nowhere else;
  a dual-brand period costs less than a recognition cliff.
- Migration is a production project with a surface count: signage 6–12 weeks, vehicle wraps tied to a 3–5 year
  cycle, packaging 3–6 months of print runs, app icons days, domains and handles hours but irreversibly.

## Techniques
1. **Mismatch audit** — three columns (what we look like / what we need to be believed about / the gap), 8–12 rows,
   each scored 1–5 on severity. Rank before drawing anything.
2. **Equity scorecard** — score every asset 1–5 for recognition and 1–5 for relevance. Keep high-recognition assets
   and change low-relevance ones.
3. **Sunset calendar** — one row per surface: owner, cost, lead time, drop-dead date. Signage and vehicles set the
   outer bound, so they set the launch date.
4. **Transitional lockup spec** — old wordmark at 60% of the new mark's height, separated by 0.5× cap height, and
   permitted only on signage, packaging, and vehicles. Not on social.

## When to Use
- Relevance scores 2 or lower, or the identity fails at a size or medium it must now work in — a 24 px app icon,
  a 3 m sign.
- After a merger, a category change, or a name change, where the old mark carries the wrong meaning.
- When applications are consistently broken while the mark still tests well: a refresh, not a rebuild.

## When to Avoid
- New leadership wanting a visible signature. That is politics; a rebuild will not fix a strategy problem.
- An identity under three years old with no structural fault; the audience has not finished learning it.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Rebuilding because the team is bored | Destroys equity and eats a year | Run the mismatch audit first |
| Launching before templates exist | Staff invent workarounds in days | Ship templates, tokens, and assets with the launch |
| Keeping everything "for continuity" | The new identity never becomes recognizable | Change the two lowest-equity invariants |
| No surface count | Quotes miss signage, vehicles, and packaging | Count surfaces before pricing |
| Old and new marks mixed forever | Reads as two companies | Put the transition end date in the guidelines |

## Practical Examples
A regional coffee roaster scored relevance 2 after twelve years. The equity scorecard kept the circular mark
(recognition 5) and the deep green (5), dropped the slab wordmark (relevance 2), and replaced it with a tighter
sans lockup at the same 1:2 proportion.

In Avnac that was three artboards: transitional lockup at 1920×1080 for signage, new lockup at 1080×1080 for
social, and a 1200×630 web header. The sunset calendar put packaging at month 9 and vehicles at month 18, which
set the launch date.
