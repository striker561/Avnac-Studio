# Rule: Postmodernism

**Domain:** history · **ID:** `107-postmodernism`

## Purpose
Use quotation, layering, and deliberate incongruity as the idea — the one movement where breaking the
rules is the content, provided you can name the rule you broke and why.

## Definition
Postmodern design (c.1970–1990) quotes historical and vernacular styles knowingly, stacks incompatible
systems in overlapping planes, re-admits decoration as meaning, and addresses the viewer as someone in
on the joke. The quotation has to be legible as a quotation.

## Core Principles
- Quotation reads as quotation only if it is dislocated: scale the borrowed style wrong, crop it, or
  set it against something that contradicts it.
- Layer, don't compose — three to five planes with different internal rules (an engraving, a grid, a
  photograph, handwriting) instead of one unified space.
- Irony needs a straight man. One element plays it completely straight so the rest reads as intent.
- Break one rule at a time, and know which rule. Chaos with no target is a mess with a theory.
- Clashing color and pattern are allowed; the clash must be composed, so collisions read as decisions.
- The audience must be in on it. Irony the viewer reads as sincerity is an error, not a style.

## Techniques
1. **Quote stack** — three layers: base (a period style — Victorian engraving, 1950s advertising),
   middle (a modern grid or a full-bleed photograph), top (vernacular or handwritten type). Each at a
   different scale.
2. **Scale dislocation** — an ornament designed for 12 pt set at 400 px; a headline dropped to 14 px.
   The wrongness is the message, so make it unmistakable rather than subtle.
3. **Register mismatch** — pair a high-contrast Didone with a pixel or mono face; set a corporate grid
   in pink and gold.
4. **Keep the body readable** — quotation belongs in display. Body copy, captions, and navigation stay
   conventional, or the piece becomes unreadable instead of critical.
5. **Copy the operation, avoid the 1980s** — copy: quotation, layering, scale dislocation, register
   clash, one straight element. Avoid: random clip-art collage, "retro 80s" gradients used as shorthand
   for postmodern, and illegibility passed off as critique.

## When to Use
- Cultural programming, music and fashion, editorial commentary about style itself.
- Brands whose audience enjoys the joke and can name the reference.

## When to Avoid
- Safety-critical, financial, health, and government contexts.
- Audiences who will read the quote as a mistake — then the client is blamed for the confusion.
- When there is no time to test whether the joke lands.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Every element clashing | No baseline left to break from | Keep one straight layer |
| Quoting a style the audience won't know | Reads as an error, not a quotation | Use broadly legible quotes, or caption them |
| Illegible type as "critique" | The reader gets nothing | Keep body copy conventional |
| Layering without depth order | Mush | Three planes maximum, explicit overlap order |
| Irony about a serious subject | Reads as contempt | Drop the irony, keep the layering |

## Practical Examples
A 1200×1600 magazine cover on "the return of the logo":

- Base: a 19th-century engraving of a laurel wreath, 1400×1400 at x=−100 y=200, `#111111` on
  `#F6F1E4`, 30% opacity.
- Middle: a 900×600 photograph at y=520 with a hard 2 px `#111111` border, unrotated — the straight
  element.
- Top: "THE RETURN" in 210 px Didone, `#E8478B`, x=90 y=140, cropped by the right edge.
- Bottom: a 12 px mono caption block at y=1500 in three conventional columns, so the joke has a floor.
