# Rule: Semantics, Syntax, and Pragmatics

**Domain:** foundations · **ID:** `48-semantics-syntax-pragmatics`

## Purpose
Give every project three questions that craft checks miss: does the piece mean the right thing, are
its parts related consistently, and does a viewer understand and act on it without being told?
Each failure is repaired at a different layer, so naming the layer is most of the fix.

## Definition
The three aspects Vignelli uses to judge design. **Semantics** is the search for meaning — the
subject, the audience, the market position, the context of use, and the roots of the problem.
**Syntax** is the consistency of relationships among the parts: type to grid, size to size, image
to image, part to whole, page to page. **Pragmatics** is whether the finished artifact works at the
point of use, with no caption explaining it and no designer in the room.

## Core Principles
- Meaning precedes form. The first work on an assignment is research into what it has to mean; a
  direction found before that is a guess with a mood board attached.
- Syntax is relational, not stylistic. A piece is syntactically consistent when the same
  relationships repeat — one heading-to-body ratio, one caption spec, one image edge — not when
  everything looks the same.
- Pragmatics is the only test that survives the studio. "Whatever we do, if not understood, fails
  to communicate and is wasted effort" (p. 14).
- Appropriateness is the semantic output. There are many ways to solve a problem, but the solution
  must be specific to this one, in medium, scale, expression, color, and texture (p. 18).
- Complexity is acceptable; complication is not. Rich content can carry many parts, but a viewer
  who cannot tell them apart has been given complication instead of richness.
- The three fail independently. A beautiful, consistent, meaningless piece is still a failure, so
  repair in the order meaning → structure → surface.

## Techniques
1. **Write the semantic brief in three lines** — what it must mean, to whom, in what context of
   use. If the third line needs a paragraph, the context is not understood yet.
2. **Audit meaning per element** — list every element and its job in one phrase. "Decoration" is a
   finding, not a category: demote it to a supporting role, or delete it.
3. **Build a consistency sheet** — the four relationships that repeat: heading size to body size,
   image edges to grid, caption spec, margin ladder. Count exceptions per page; more than one means
   the syntax is broken, not expressive.
4. **Run the pragmatic test at final context** — final size, final distance, five seconds, then ask
   "what does it say, and what do you do next?". If the viewer needs your rationale, the artifact
   failed.
5. **Repair in layer order** — content and copy first, structure second, surface last. Re-kerning a
   page whose message is wrong is wasted craft.
6. **Close with three answers** — one line each in the delivery note: the meaning you designed for,
   the relationships you fixed, the comprehension you tested.

## When to Use
- At kickoff, to turn a request into a problem with a specific solution.
- Before delivery, when the work is technically clean but feels wrong: the defect is usually
  semantic or syntactic, not a matter of taste.
- When a client asks for a style or a reference ("make it like this"): the semantic question is
  what that style is doing for them.

## When to Avoid
- Purely expressive work where a plurality of readings is the point — Vignelli treats ambiguity as
  a controlled spice, not a default.
- A one-line banner or a single icon: run the three questions in your head, not as a written audit.
- Do not use "it's conceptual" to defend work nobody understands; that is a pragmatic failure in a
  semantic costume.

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Starting from references | Style chosen before meaning is a costume | Write the three-line semantic brief first |
| Consistency as an end in itself | A rigid system flattens content that needs different treatment | Repeat relationships, vary composition |
| Testing comprehension on yourself | You know what it means; the viewer does not | Five-second test with someone outside the project |
| Explaining the work instead of fixing it | The rationale lives in the meeting, not in the artifact | Move the explanation into the design, or remove the need |
| Adding elements to clarify | Each addition adds syntax to decode | Subtract, then re-test |

## Practical Examples
A community cinema asks for a season poster. The obvious move is an art-house look: a grainy
still, tiny type, a narrow serif.

Semantics: the audience is first-time viewers, not cinephiles, and the job is to make the programme
feel approachable. Those signals work against the meaning, so they go. The headline becomes
"NEVER SEEN IT? START HERE." at 64 px, the season name 28 px, dates 20 px.

Syntax: one heading ratio (64/28/20), one image treatment (duotone in the two brand inks), image
always in the bottom 40%, margins 8% of the short edge. Across six posters only the photograph and
the film names change.

Pragmatics: at 25% zoom the headline and the first date still read in three seconds; at 2 m in the
foyer the season name is legible before the film titles. A decorative film-strip border read as a
barcode at thumbnail size; it was cut in the meaning audit, not retouched later.
